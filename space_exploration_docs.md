## 1. What is a rocket
A rocket is a vehicle that generates thrust by expelling mass (usually
hot exhaust gas) at high speed in the opposite direction, following
Newton's third law of motion. Unlike jet engines, rockets carry their
own oxidizer, which allows them to operate outside Earth's atmosphere,
including in the vacuum of space.

## 2. Orbital velocity
Orbital velocity is the speed an object needs to maintain a stable
orbit around a celestial body. For a low Earth orbit, this is roughly
7.8 kilometers per second. If a spacecraft goes slower, gravity pulls
it back down; if it goes fast enough (escape velocity), it can leave
the body's gravitational field entirely.

## 3. Satellites
A satellite is any object that orbits a larger body in space, whether
natural (like the Moon) or artificial (built and launched by humans).
Artificial satellites are used for communications, GPS navigation,
weather forecasting, Earth observation, and scientific research.

## 4. The International Space Station
The International Space Station (ISS) is a habitable satellite in low
Earth orbit, jointly operated by multiple space agencies including
NASA, Roscosmos, and ESA. It has hosted a continuous human presence
since November 2000 and serves as a microgravity research laboratory.

## 5. Mars missions
Mars has been a major target for robotic exploration due to evidence
of past water and its potential to have hosted microbial life. Rovers
like NASA's Curiosity and Perseverance have driven across the Martian
surface, analyzing rock samples and searching for signs of ancient
habitability.

## 6. Reusable rockets
Reusable rockets, pioneered at scale by companies like SpaceX, are
designed to land back on Earth after launch instead of being
discarded. By reflying the same booster stage multiple times, launch
costs can be reduced dramatically compared to traditional expendable
rockets.

## 7. The Moon landing (Apollo program)
The Apollo program's Apollo 11 mission achieved the first crewed Moon
landing on July 20, 1969, when astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz
Aldrin walked on the lunar surface while Michael Collins orbited
above in the command module. It remains one of the most significant
achievements in space exploration history.

## 8. Space telescopes
Space telescopes, such as the Hubble Space Telescope and the James
Webb Space Telescope, observe the universe from above Earth's
atmosphere, avoiding the blurring and light pollution that limit
ground-based observatories. This allows them to capture sharper
images and detect wavelengths of light, like infrared, that the
atmosphere would otherwise block.

## 9. Rocket propulsion types
Most rockets today use chemical propulsion, burning liquid or solid
fuel with an oxidizer to produce thrust. Alternative propulsion
methods, such as ion thrusters, produce much lower thrust but are far
more fuel-efficient, making them useful for long-duration deep space
missions where high acceleration is not required.

## 10. Space debris
Space debris consists of defunct satellites, spent rocket stages, and
fragments from collisions or explosions that remain in orbit around
Earth. As the amount of debris grows, it increases the risk of
collisions with active satellites and spacecraft, a concern known as
the Kessler syndrome.
